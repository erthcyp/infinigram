The questionWhy this needs answering carefully

Every review of this project will begin the same way. HALoGEN already classifies hallucinations by their relationship to pretraining data. It is from a strong group, it is well cited, and its Type A, B and C scheme is exactly the distinction we are built around. So what is left for us?

A vague answer loses the room. "We go deeper" or "we do it at scale" invites the follow up question we would then fail. The answer has to be specific enough that someone can check it.

What follows is that answer. It rests on reading HALoGEN closely enough to see which tool it used, which corpora it actually searched, and what those choices made impossible.

Prior workWhat HALoGEN actually did

HALoGEN is a broad hallucination benchmark. 10,923 prompts across nine domains, roughly 150,000 generations from fourteen models, every generation broken into atomic facts and each fact checked automatically against an authoritative source. That part is excellent and we borrow from it directly.

Then, separately, it does the thing nobody else does. It asks where the wrong fact came from.

Type A   the correct fact was in the pretraining data, recall failed
Type B   the erroneous fact was itself in the pretraining data
Type C   neither was there, the model over generalised or invented

Its findings are real and useful. Up to 72 percent of hallucinated code package names appear verbatim in pretraining corpora. Wrong senator alma maters occur even though the correct one is present. Impossible historical meetings have almost no corpus basis at all.

Three constraints on what that amounts to

It is an analysis, not a measurement. The A, B and C attribution is applied to selected domains as a discussion section. It is not a label carried by every instance. Download HALoGEN today and you get hallucination scores, not provenance labels.

Presence is recorded as a boolean. Whether a fact appeared, not how often. The information that distinguishes appearing three times from appearing twelve thousand times is discarded.

Attribution was only possible where the data is public. Fourteen models were evaluated. Most do not publish their corpus.

The paper says the third point itself, in its limitations:

"many of the LLMs examined in this work do not release their data sources, limiting the accuracy of our attributions. This points toward the need for open language models which enable transparent inspection of pretraining data." HALoGEN, limitations

That sentence is the strongest argument for our scope that exists, and we did not write it. HALoGEN says the fix is open language models. We take every open language model that qualifies, which happens to be all five in the world whose corpus is both published and indexed.

And its future work names the two things it could not reach:

"Future work would construct causal frameworks, to study counterfactual questions about the inclusion of specific datapoints and their effect on specific model hallucinations... while we search for facts as they are stated in model responses, these facts could be present implicitly in pretraining corpora." HALoGEN, future work

Difference oneThe tool gap, and why it is not cosmetic

HALoGEN searched training data with WIMBD. We use infini-gram. Both come from the same lab, and it would be easy to treat this as a version upgrade. It is not.

The consequence

Relating hallucination rate to how often a fact appeared requires occurrence counts. WIMBD returns document frequency, and its large n-gram counting is approximate by design.

So the frequency curve, which is the measurement that opens the entire training process axis for us, was not available to HALoGEN with the tool it used. This is not us having a newer toy. It is a capability that did not exist in their setup.

The cost of the newer tool is a problem WIMBD never had, because it matched text rather than tokens. Token boundary contamination is real, it is structural, and we had to design a procedure for it from scratch since no prior work had needed one. That investigation is written up separately, and the procedure is itself a contribution.

Four pairs, all Python API names, which is where C4 is weakest since it is filtered web text with almost no source code. For claims about people or papers the gap would likely be narrower. BoundariesWhat we do not claim

Overstating here is the fastest way to lose credibility on everything above, so these belong in the same document as the contributions.

  • We do not establish cause. Proving that removing a document removes an error requires retraining the model, which is beyond any budget relevant to this study. We locate where an error is addressable. That is a weaker claim than causation and a stronger one than anything else on offer.
  • We are smaller than HALoGEN. Five models against fourteen, three tasks against nine. We go narrow and deep on the one question they left open, and we should say so rather than imply breadth we do not have.
  • We build no new verifiers. The detection stage follows their approach with their sources.
  • Absence is an upper bound. Exact string search cannot find a fact stated in different words, so every "not in the data" label carries that caveat and is reported as a bound.
  • Our index coverage is not complete. 98.7 percent of tokens for OLMo 2, 93 percent for DCLM. The fraction is attached to every figure we report.

The gap checkDid anyone already follow up

Our positioning depends on a negative claim, and negative claims are the ones reviewers check first. So it was worth searching properly rather than assuming.

HALoGEN appeared in January 2025 and is roughly twenty months old. Searching for papers that adopt its A, B and C taxonomy or perform pretraining corpus attribution of hallucinations turned up around a dozen citing papers, all of which cite it in passing in related work or in a comparison table. None adopts the taxonomy. None reuses its prompts or verifiers. None does corpus attribution at scale.

The nearest thing is a paper that explicitly notes HALoGEN "distinguishes hallucinations based on whether incorrect facts were present during pretraining" and then cites it in order to argue against it, proposing its own framework instead.

Verify this before it goes in the paper

The citation APIs at Semantic Scholar and OpenAlex were unreachable from the environment where this search ran, so the count above comes from full text search coverage rather than from a citation list. It is a floor, not a census.

Open the Semantic Scholar page for arXiv:2501.08292 by hand and read the citing papers before writing "no descendants" anywhere. It takes five minutes and it is the single easiest thing for a reviewer to check.

Adjacent workTwo papers we have to cite

Lexical training data coverage, arXiv:2511.17946

The closest work by method. It builds suffix arrays over RedPajama's 1.3 trillion tokens and measures how training data coverage relates to hallucination, on RedPajama-INCITE 3B and 7B, which is one of our own five subject models.

It does something different with it. Coverage becomes a feature for detecting whether an answer is likely hallucinated, not a label for where the error came from. It does not search for the correct form, so it cannot distinguish a recall failure from a knowledge gap. It does not cite HALoGEN and does not use A, B or C.

Two of its numbers matter to us regardless. Occurrence features alone reach only about 0.6 AUROC as a detector. And 21 percent of 4-grams and 44 percent of 5-grams in NQ-Open never appear in the training data at all, which is advance warning that our "not in the data" category will be larger than intuition suggests.

Cite it and distinguish it explicitly, or a reviewer will ask whether we are duplicating it.

Scalable influence and fact tracing, arXiv:2410.17413

Influence functions on an 8B model over 160 billion pretraining tokens. This is the real instrument for causal attribution, the thing we said is out of reach, done by a group that could reach it.

Its central finding is the one we should quote against ourselves: causal influence and factual attribution do not agree. Retrieval finds the documents that contain a fact, while influence functions point at a different set of documents that actually drove the output.

Use this in our limitations, not against us

Raise it ourselves. Work that measures causal influence directly shows it diverges from finding where a fact is stated, which is why we call our output an intervention point rather than a cause. Citing the paper that could undermine a careless version of our claim is what makes the careful version credible.

The short versionIn one paragraph

HALoGEN asked the right question and answered it with the tool available at the time. WIMBD tells you whether a string appears and in how many documents; it cannot give exact occurrence counts at arbitrary length, so the frequency signal was closed to them, and for two of three model families they had to search a substitute corpus. infini-gram removes both constraints. We take the question they opened, give it the instrument it needed, apply it to every instance rather than a case study, keep the counts as numbers rather than as yes and no, add repeated sampling so training and inference can be separated, and cross the result against the failure mode to produce a table nobody has published. We also measure what their substitution cost, since that is now something we can measure and they could not.

If someone gets only one sentence, make it the second half of the first line. The frequency signal was closed to them. Everything else follows from that.
